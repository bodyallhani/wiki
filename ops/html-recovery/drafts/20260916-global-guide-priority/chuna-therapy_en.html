Chuna in Suwon: how Bodyall selects myofascial care, MET and SART

Recurring neck or back pain, and unsure which type of Chuna fits?

At Bodyall Korean Medicine Clinic in Ingye-dong, Suwon, Chief Director Donghae Lee first checks numbness, weakness and medical history, then compares neck, back and pelvic movement with difficult daily tasks. Myofascial Chuna and MET for muscle tension are distinguished from corrective Chuna and SART for joint movement.

Bodyall explains the site, direction and intensity of correction. When SART is suitable, pelvic support using the Insanggi device, sacroiliac and spinal joint mobilisation, and optional correction tools are selected from the examination findings. The same neck turn, sit-to-stand or walk is then checked again.

Signs that need medical assessment before a Chuna appointment

New bladder or bowel dysfunction, altered sensation around the perineum, weakness in both legs or pain after major trauma needs emergency assessment. Fever or chills with rapidly worsening pain, and progressive arm or leg weakness, also need prompt assessment. Call 119 in Korea for a sudden severe headache or neck pain with speech, vision or balance changes.

What guides treatment selection at Bodyall?

Start with one or two difficult tasks: turning the neck, prolonged sitting, standing from a chair or walking. These are assessed under comparable conditions before and after care, together with sensation, strength and relevant previous investigations.

  1. Myofascial Chuna and MET: Muscle tension and restricted movement guide the choice of relaxation methods. MET involves the patient contracting and relaxing muscles within a tolerable, cooperative range.
  2. Joint mobilisation and conditional SART: When spinal or pelvic joint movement also needs attention and there are no contraindications, care may combine Insanggi pelvic support, sacroiliac and spinal mobilisation, manual correction and optional tools. The examination determines site, direction and intensity; the same sequence is not applied to everyone.
  3. Relevant investigation and referral: Bodyall provides ultrasound. When the examination suggests a need to assess muscles, tendons or other soft tissue, the purpose is explained. Ultrasound alone does not establish spinal canal, disc or nerve compression diagnoses; neurological findings or suspected other disease may require further investigations or specialist assessment.
  4. Repeat the same functional check: Pain, numbness and range are checked again in the same task after care. Changes in sitting, walking and sleep then guide exercise, daily movement advice and the site and intensity of subsequent treatment.

SART (Spine Alignment Restoration Therapy) is Bodyall's Chuna and spinal-correction care protocol. It combines pelvic support with the Insanggi device, mobilisation of the sacroiliac joints and individual spinal segments, and corrective instruments selected from the examination findings. The aim is to improve daily movements limited by pain.

Questions about Chuna consultations

What is checked before considering Chuna?

Onset, difficult movements, numbness, weakness, medical history and medicines are checked first. Bodyall combines posture, spinal and pelvic palpation, active and passive range, and walking assessment. A single posture or alignment finding does not establish the cause of pain or the likely treatment effect.

How do myofascial Chuna and MET differ from SART at Bodyall?

Myofascial Chuna and MET address muscle tension and movement control. SART is considered when spinal or pelvic joint movement also needs attention and correction is suitable. Examination guides Insanggi pelvic support, joint mobilisation, optional tools, treatment site and intensity, followed by reassessment of the same daily task.

What should I check about Chuna costs and insurance?

National Health Insurance coverage and the copayment depend on diagnosis, the procedure and benefit criteria. Private insurance reimbursement also depends on the policy and insurer assessment. Ask for itemised costs and coverage for the proposed care. The name SART does not make every component subject to the same insurance criteria; confirm the actual procedures separately.

What the related research shows—and its limits

A 2019 review of 47 trials involving 9,211 adults with chronic low-back pain examined pain and function at 1, 6 and 12 months. Short-term pain outcomes did not differ significantly from recommended care; function showed a small benefit. Studies varied, and only about half assessed adverse events. Most reported events were transient musculoskeletal symptoms, but one study reported a serious event considered possibly related to manipulation. [1]

A 2022 trial assigned 201 adults with subacute or chronic low-back pain to 12 weeks of multidisciplinary integrative or chiropractic care, with follow-up to 52 weeks. Integrative care had a small 0.5-point advantage on a 0–10 pain scale across weeks 4–52; the weeks 4–12 difference was not significant. Some functional and satisfaction measures differed, but medication use and quality of life did not. No treatment-related serious events occurred; temporary pain increases occurred in both groups. [2]

What to disclose before care, and what to monitor afterward

Tell the clinician about fractures, severe osteoporosis, recent surgery, cancer or infection, anticoagulants, pregnancy or postpartum recovery, and previous reactions to manual care. Temporary soreness, stiffness or headache may follow manipulation; rare serious neurological or spinal problems have also been reported. New neurological symptoms or continuing deterioration need reassessment before treatment is repeated.

Cervical manipulation has been associated with neck artery injury and stroke, although causation is debated. Discuss this potential risk and other options before neck manipulation.
