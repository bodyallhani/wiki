Fatigue that persists despite rest: assessing pelvic and lumbosacral symptoms alongside herbal care

💡 Q. Why consider spinal and pelvic symptoms when assessing persistent fatigue?

When fatigue occurs with lower-back or pelvic pain, the causes of fatigue and the accompanying musculoskeletal symptoms need separate assessment. Bodyall Korean Medicine Clinic considers herbal care alongside spinal care, including SART, where relevant. However, spinal or pelvic misalignment has not been established as a cause of chronic fatigue, and SART has not been established as a treatment for chronic fatigue.

1. Possible causes to assess when fatigue persists

Fatigue can relate to sleep problems, anemia, thyroid disease, ongoing pain or medicines. Seek medical assessment if rest does not help, and do not stop medicines on your own. Persistent fatigue alone does not establish a diagnosis of myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) (MedlinePlus: Fatigue).

Mention lower-back or pelvic pain, or discomfort with movement, as a separate concern. The manual-therapy study discussed here involved women with menstrual pain; it cannot support a claim that pelvic correction restores blood flow or mitochondrial ATP production to relieve fatigue.

2. Considering spinal care and an herbal prescription

Two topics to discuss at the consultation

A consultation can address the causes of fatigue and whether an herbal prescription is appropriate, while separately considering accompanying lower-back or pelvic symptoms and whether spinal care is needed.

What does Bodyall assess when lower-back or pelvic symptoms coexist?

Bodyall focuses on Chuna and spinal care and uses biomechanical spatial decompression Chuna (SART Protocol) as a clinical protocol. SART stands for Spine Alignment Restoration Therapy. The following spinal and pelvic care process was described by the clinic; it is distinct from assessing the causes of fatigue or deciding whether to prescribe herbal medicine.

Assessment: The clinic uses Exbody to assess overall posture and left–right balance, together with spinal and pelvic palpation, active and passive range of motion, gait and key symptoms. These findings are considered together to determine the direction of treatment; posture analysis alone does not determine that decision.

Treatment approach: The sequence is to address space and loading, refine alignment, immediately reassess through the patient's active movement, and provide education about everyday movements. The clinic uses cheopjeon and insanggi to adjust weight-bearing through the pelvis and assess movement of the pelvis, sacroiliac joints and spine. When needed, a medical hammer called jinjeong and adjustment tools are used. Here, addressing space means managing loading and compression to facilitate physiological joint movement; it does not mean restoring a disc or permanently enlarging joint spaces.

Checking the response: Immediately after treatment, the patient repeats the movement that caused discomfort. The practitioner checks the reproduction of pain or tingling and changes in range of motion. The treatment area, intensity, sequence and everyday-movement education are adjusted according to the response at each visit. These immediate movement changes do not demonstrate improvement in chronic fatigue or a long-term treatment effect.

If fatigue occurs alongside lower-back or pelvic discomfort during movement, ask whether this musculoskeletal assessment is appropriate in addition to consultation about fatigue and herbal care. General spinal manipulation can cause temporary pain, stiffness or headache, and serious adverse events have rarely been reported. Tell the practitioner about your medical history and medicines in advance (NCCIH: Spinal Manipulation).

If considering herbal medicines such as Gongjin-Dan or Gyeongok-Go, discuss whether the prescription suits your symptoms and health. Disclose medicines, underlying conditions, and pregnancy or breastfeeding, and ask about directions and precautions. Herbal care does not replace investigations into the cause of symptoms or other necessary medical care (NCCIH guidance on herbal care).

3. What the related studies actually found

Scope of the manual-therapy study: This trial compared high-velocity, low-amplitude (HVLA) sacroiliac joint manipulation with superficial heat in women with primary dysmenorrhea. The final analysis included 35 participants (18 and 17), and the primary outcome was heart rate variability (HRV). An autonomic balance measure derived from HRV differed between groups at week 4, but the sacroiliac pressure-pain threshold did not differ significantly. Pain on a visual analogue scale (VAS) decreased in both groups, without a significant group-by-time interaction. Menstrual distress questionnaire (MMDQ) scores favored manipulation at weeks 4 and 8 [1]. This did not test people with chronic fatigue, Bodyall's SART, or combined herbal and manual treatment, so it is limited background evidence from a related field.

Gongjin-Dan and Ssanghwa-Tang trial: no significant benefit over placebo on the primary fatigue outcome

Participants and interventions: Ninety adults with fatigue of unclear cause for at least 6 months and a mean Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS) score of at least 4 were allocated to three groups of 30. The Gongjin-Dan group received one pill daily plus Ssanghwa-Tang placebo; the Ssanghwa-Tang group received three sachets daily plus Gongjin-Dan placebo; the control group received both placebos. Treatment lasted 4 weeks, with follow-up to week 6 [2].

Primary outcome: Change in FSS at week 4 did not differ significantly from placebo in either treatment group. The mean difference versus placebo was −0.19 points for Gongjin-Dan (97.5% confidence interval −0.64 to 0.26; p=0.4080) and −0.18 for Ssanghwa-Tang (−0.58 to 0.23; p=0.3877) [2].

Secondary outcomes: There were also no significant between-group differences in FSS at week 6 or in MFI-20 and CFQ fatigue measures at weeks 4 and 6. Gongjin-Dan's SF-36 social-functioning score at week 4 was 10.43 points higher than placebo (95% confidence interval 2.41 to 18.44), but this is a quality-of-life subscale. Fewer participants in the Ssanghwa-Tang group met the SOFA/GP fatigue criteria at week 4 than in the control group. Measured fatigue-related biomarkers showed no significant between-group differences [2].

Corrigendum: A 2025 correction reversed the original interpretation that the Ssanghwa-Tang group had higher SF-36 role-emotional scores at weeks 4 and 6 and physical-functioning scores at week 6 than the control group. Those scores were actually lower. Gongjin-Dan's social-functioning finding and the primary FSS findings were unchanged. Read the corrigendum [3]

Safety and limitations: Temporary adverse events, including heartburn and nausea, were reported; there were no serious intervention-related adverse events. Laboratory values showed no clinically important abnormalities. Four weeks of treatment and 6 weeks of observation cannot establish long-term safety or effectiveness for everyone with fatigue. Results for the trial products cannot be directly applied to Bodyall prescriptions or to combining them with SART [2].

4. Bodyall's approach to a consultation for persistent fatigue

💡 Q. Must I receive both herbal care and SART at Bodyall?

Considering both approaches does not mean that everyone must receive both treatments. An herbal consultation can address prescription suitability; a spinal consultation can address accompanying musculoskeletal problems and whether treatment is needed. The studies discussed here do not establish that SART combined with herbal medicine is superior to either treatment alone.

What should I prepare before a first consultation?

Bring notes on when fatigue began, sleep and accompanying symptoms, medicines and previous test results. Mention any lower-back or pelvic pain. This is general preparation guidance; the actual history-taking and tests depend on the clinical assessment (MedlinePlus: Fatigue).

When is further medical assessment needed?

Seek medical assessment for unexplained fatigue accompanied by fever, unintended weight loss or recurring sweats. Confusion or a marked reduction in urine output calls for prompt medical attention. Depending on the examination, tests for anemia, blood glucose, thyroid, liver or kidney function may be considered at a medical facility able to provide the necessary assessment (MedlinePlus: Fatigue).

💡 Q. What changes should I record after a visit?

Record fatigue severity, sleep and difficulties with daily activities for follow-up. In Bodyall's spinal care, the same movement is repeated immediately after treatment to check pain, tingling and range of motion and guide adjustments. The course of fatigue and the musculoskeletal response are assessed separately. If symptoms persist or worsen, discuss reassessing their causes and the treatment plan.
