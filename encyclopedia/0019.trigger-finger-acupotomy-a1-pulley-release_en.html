Trigger Finger in Suwon: Locking-Finger Assessment and Acupotomy Consultation

💡 Q. What does Bodyall check when a finger catches or locks?

Bodyall first identifies the painful finger and exact site, reproduces catching during flexion-extension, and checks tenderness or a nodule over the palmar A1 pulley. Joint disease, Dupuytren contracture and tendon injury are considered alongside diabetes, rheumatic disease, medicines and prior treatment response before deciding whether acupotomy is appropriate.

Why choose Bodyall: Chief Director Donghae Lee does not select the procedure first. He explains the differential, alternatives and invasive risks. If treatment is chosen, the same flexion-extension and locking movement is reassessed to guide the next plan.

Who may benefit from this decision pathway

This pathway may suit people with clicking or catching, morning stiffness, a finger that sometimes needs the other hand to straighten, or palm-side pain that limits gripping, work or daily tasks. Someone in Suwon who wants trigger finger differentiated from other finger problems before comparing conservative care, injection or surgical consultation and acupotomy may reasonably consider Bodyall Korean Medicine Clinic.

Next step: note when symptoms are worst, which movement reproduces catching, symptom duration, prior care and current medicines. Sudden loss of motion or deformity after trauma, rapidly worsening redness, warmth, swelling or fever, or new numbness or weakness may require other assessment first.

What trigger finger is—and what can mimic it

Trigger finger is stenosing flexor tenosynovitis in which a flexor tendon catches while passing through the palmar A1 pulley, causing pain, clicking or locking. Assessment combines symptom location, reproduced catching, A1 pulley tenderness or nodularity, and differences between active and passive flexion-extension. Ultrasound may support an unclear diagnosis or suspected structural alternative; quantitative imaging is not mandatory for every patient.

Pain centered on the joint line, a cord-like palmar contracture, or sudden flexion-extension loss after injury may point elsewhere. Diabetes, rheumatic disease and recurrent symptoms in several digits also inform treatment selection and prognosis discussions.

How Bodyall decides whether acupotomy fits

  1. Confirm the working diagnosis: assess catching site and movement, A1 pulley tenderness or nodularity, active and passive range of motion, and relevant joint, tendon and neurologic findings.
  2. Compare alternatives: review duration, functional limits and response to activity modification, splinting, medicine or injection, and explain when orthopedic surgical consultation may be appropriate. A randomized trial compared corticosteroid injection, percutaneous release and open surgery, so acupotomy is not presented as the only option.[3]
  3. Select acupotomy only when indicated: consider it individually when the A1 pulley is a plausible target and the patient understands invasiveness, alternatives and risk. The actual target and method depend on examination.
  4. Reassess the same function: after treatment, repeat the same flexion, extension and grip that produced catching. Limited response or worsening prompts diagnostic and plan reassessment.

Acupotomy is an invasive procedure that inserts a flat-tipped instrument through the skin; it is not ordinary acupuncture. Possible harms include injury to a digital nerve, digital artery or flexor tendon, bleeding, infection, and temporary pain or swelling. Anticoagulants, antiplatelet drugs, bleeding disorders, infection and allergies should be disclosed.

What the cited acupotomy papers directly show

Ou and colleagues reported one 49-year-old woman with trigger thumb treated by ultrasound-guided longitudinal A1 pulley release, with post-procedure changes in pain, clicking and range of motion. The report shows procedural possibility, but one thumb case cannot establish effectiveness or safety for other digits or typical patients, and it does not validate outcomes at Bodyall.[1]

The 2019 paper by Jia and colleagues is a systematic-review protocol describing how acupotomy studies would be collected and assessed. It reports planned methods and outcomes, not completed review results or a confirmed conclusion on effectiveness or safety.[2]

Frequently asked questions

Why does trigger finger catch?

Clicking, pain or locking can occur when a flexor tendon catches while passing through a narrowed segment of the A1 pulley. Location and reproduced movement help distinguish it from joint or other tendon problems.

When does Bodyall consider acupotomy?

It is considered individually after confirming whether the A1 pulley is a plausible target and reviewing functional limits, prior conservative response, alternatives and invasive risks.

What are the important acupotomy risks?

Possible harms include injury to a digital nerve, digital artery or flexor tendon, bleeding, infection and temporary pain or swelling. Medicines and bleeding, infection and allergy information should be disclosed.

Will acupotomy immediately return my finger to normal?

That cannot be guaranteed. The cited clinical acupotomy evidence is one trigger-thumb case, while the other paper is a protocol without results. The same movement is reassessed and the plan adjusted to the response.
