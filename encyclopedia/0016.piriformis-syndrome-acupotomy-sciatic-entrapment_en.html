Buttock Pain and Leg Tingling in Suwon: Piriformis Syndrome Assessment with Acupotomy and Chuna

💡 Q. My buttock and leg tingle when I sit. Is this piriformis syndrome?

Deep buttock pain and sitting-related tingling are clues, but symptoms overlap with lumbar disc or stenosis-related radiculopathy, hip and sacroiliac disorders, and proximal hamstring problems. Bodyall compares lumbar and hip motion, strength, sensation, reflexes, neural tension, deep-gluteal tenderness, and symptom-provoking tasks rather than diagnosing from one test.

How Bodyall defines the treatment scope: Chief Director Donghae Lee separates a local deep-gluteal target from spinal or pelvic compensation and assigns different roles to myofascial Chuna, Acupotomy, and SART. Acupotomy is considered only when a local target is clear and appropriate after explaining deep anatomy, invasive risks, and alternatives. Sitting, walking, stairs, and hip rotation are repeated after care.

How piriformis syndrome is differentiated

Deep gluteal syndrome describes non-discogenic sciatic-nerve irritation or entrapment in the deep gluteal space; the piriformis is one possible cause. A systematic review found that common diagnostic pathways combined a history of posterior hip or radiating pain and sitting difficulty, deep-gluteal tenderness and provocative tests, and pelvic or spinal imaging. No single maneuver or image is a definitive test.[1]

Bodyall first checks strength, sensation, reflexes, and lumbar neural-tension signs, then compares hip rotation, sacroiliac loading, deep-gluteal tenderness, and symptom reproduction. Imaging or another referral is discussed when indicated. An anatomic variant may explain susceptibility but is not declared causal without matching symptoms and examination.

How Bodyall selects Acupotomy, Chuna, and SART

  1. Screen risk and lumbar causes: identify progressive neurologic signs, possible cauda equina syndrome, trauma, infection, or vascular concerns, then compare lumbar radiculopathy.
  2. Separate local and linked movement findings: record whether deep-gluteal palpation, hip rotation, or sitting reproduces symptoms and whether lumbar, pelvic, or gait changes modify them.
  3. Assign treatment roles: myofascial Chuna addresses a local soft-tissue finding. Corrective Chuna including SART is considered separately when spinal, sacroiliac, or pelvic compensation is found. Acupotomy for a persistent local target is discussed only after the sciatic nerve and vascular location, bleeding, infection and nerve-injury risks, and alternatives are explained.
  4. Reassess the same function: repeat sitting tolerance, walking, stairs, hip rotation, and tingling distribution under comparable conditions. An inadequate or worsening response prompts reconsideration of diagnosis, imaging, injection, or specialist referral.

What the two surgical papers do—and do not—show

The 2022 paper reports two surgically treated refractory cases with rare sciatic-nerve variation. The 2024 paper also examines surgical decompression around the sacrotuberous ligament and piriformis.[2][3] They help describe deep-gluteal anatomy and refractory entrapment, but do not establish Acupotomy adhesiolysis, combined SART benefit, or Bodyall outcomes.

Bodyall's choice rationale is therefore the care process: the same chief director differentiates lumbar, hip, sacroiliac, and deep-gluteal sources; separates local soft-tissue treatment from spinal or pelvic correction; selects only the indicated intervention; and follows the same function over time.

Frequently asked questions

Does a positive FAIR or Pace test confirm piriformis syndrome?

No. It is a clue to deep-gluteal pain and must be compared with lumbar radiculopathy and hip or sacroiliac causes.

Does Acupotomy certainly release sciatic adhesions?

That is not claimed. The two cited papers are surgical studies, not Acupotomy-effect studies. Bodyall assesses the local target and invasive risks separately.

When is SART considered?

Only when examination finds lumbar, sacroiliac, or pelvic compensation separate from the local deep-gluteal problem.

Which signs require prompt evaluation?

Bladder or bowel dysfunction, saddle numbness, progressive leg weakness, fever or major trauma, marked unilateral leg swelling, chest pain, or shortness of breath warrants prompt evaluation.
