Leg tingling when you stand up: is an MRI report of a “herniated disc” enough?

If low-back or buttock pain travels into your leg, or tingling worsens when you rise from sitting in Suwon

Lumbar nerve-root symptoms should be considered, but an MRI phrase such as “disc herniation” cannot determine the cause or treatment by itself. At Bodyall Korean Medicine Clinic, Chief Director Donghae Lee compares the symptom path, strength, sensation and reflexes with lumbar, hip and sacroiliac movement. Myofascial Chuna and corrective Chuna/SART are separated only when conservative care is appropriate, and the same difficult task—such as rising or walking—is retested. Warning signs or an image–symptom mismatch take priority for imaging or specialist assessment.

How are imaging and leg symptoms matched?

A herniated disc may irritate or compress a nerve root, producing pain or tingling from the low back or buttock into a leg, with altered sensation, weakness or reduced reflexes. Yet an imaging finding may not match the current symptoms, and hip, sacroiliac or peripheral-nerve problems can feel similar. The symptom route, neurological examination, provocative tests such as straight-leg raise or slump, and lumbar-pelvic movement therefore need to be interpreted together.

First determine whether conservative care is appropriate

The 2024 WFNS Spine Committee recommendations support conservative management—including activity modification, medication and physical therapy—as first-line care for lumbar disc herniation when cauda equina syndrome and neurological deficit are absent.[1] This does not mean manipulation suits everyone; it supports screening neurological status and warning signs first.

At Bodyall, myofascial Chuna is considered when soft-tissue tension predominates, while corrective Chuna or SART may be selected when lumbar-pelvic or sacroiliac movement is also involved. When indicated, SART uses a reverse-gravity treatment device (insanggi) to support the pelvis against gravity and create the position and working space needed to address lumbar-pelvic segmental movement. Joint mobilisation is then directed to the findings identified on examination, and the same task and leg symptom are compared before and after care.

What do the cited studies actually show?

A 2018 pre–post study treated 20 people who had MRI-confirmed lumbar disc herniation plus sacroiliac hypomobility, without a control group. Although some changes were statistically significant, pain and disability changes did not reach the study's minimum clinically important thresholds, and the authors stated that the design could not support clinical efficacy.[2] A 2014 prospective cohort observed patient-reported improvement over one year in selected MRI-confirmed patients, but it was not a randomized comparison; neither study examined SART directly.[3] Bodyall uses these studies to inform who may be considered for general manual care and how progress is observed, while SART is selected from the individual examination.

Signs that need urgent assessment first

Rapidly worsening leg weakness, symptoms in both legs, saddle numbness, inability to pass urine, new urinary or bowel incontinence require immediate assessment for cauda equina syndrome. Major trauma, fever, a history of cancer, unexplained weight loss or severe systemic night pain also take priority over manual care.

Preparing for your first visit

Bring the MRI or X-ray report and images, your medication list, and note which leg and toes the pain or tingling reaches. Identify the task that reproduces it most clearly—rising from a chair, coughing, walking or bending—so it can be compared before and after care.
