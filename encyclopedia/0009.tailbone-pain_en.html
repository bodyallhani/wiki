Tailbone pain when sitting or standing: is it the coccyx, lower back or pelvis?

If tailbone pain persists after a fall or prolonged sitting in Suwon

Start with when it began, focal coccygeal tenderness and how sitting and standing change it, rather than assuming a “misaligned pelvis.” At Bodyall Korean Medicine Clinic, Chief Director Donghae Lee directly reviews trauma, childbirth, prolonged sitting, skin and neurological findings, then compares coccygeal tenderness with lumbar, sacroiliac, hip and pelvic-floor movement in one visit. Load modification, acupuncture or pharmacopuncture, myofascial Chuna, corrective Chuna/conditional SART and acupotomy are assigned separate purposes, followed by retesting the same sitting, sit-to-stand task and tenderness.

Which symptoms and findings are assessed together?

Coccydynia commonly causes focal pain and tenderness that worsens with prolonged sitting, leaning back or rising from a chair. Pain near the tailbone is not always a coccygeal-alignment problem. Falls, childbirth, repetitive load and the exact pain location are reviewed, while lumbar referred pain, sacroiliac, hip, pelvic-floor, skin infection or mass and neurological causes are distinguished. When examination and routine imaging do not explain persistent symptoms, or when trauma, night pain or systemic symptoms are present, seated-versus-standing dynamic radiographs, MRI or other indicated testing can be coordinated.[1]

Pelvic movement is tested for relevance, not presumed to be the cause

The coccyx lies below the sacrum and receives pelvic-floor muscle and ligament attachments. Lumbar or pelvic movement can influence pain, but that does not establish coccygeal displacement or pelvic malalignment. Lumbar flexion and extension, single-leg loading and hip movement are checked separately to see whether they reproduce or change the familiar pain.

How does Bodyall distinguish coccygeal from lumbar-pelvic pain?

Bodyall Korean Medicine Clinic has cared for 40,000 cumulative correction-care patients through December 2025 (clinic count). The chief director compares focal coccygeal findings with lumbar, sacroiliac, hip, pelvic-floor and neurological findings, then selects care for the identified region and purpose. Pain-free sitting time, the sit-to-stand task and focal tenderness are retested after care. A limited response or a new warning sign leads to imaging and the appropriate pain-medicine, orthopaedic, gynaecological or other review.

When lumbar or pelvic movement changes the familiar pain and a relevant restriction is found, myofascial Chuna or corrective Chuna/SART may be considered. When indicated, SART uses a reverse-gravity treatment device (insanggi) to support the pelvis against gravity and create the position and working space needed for sacroiliac and lumbar segmental mobilisation. Acupotomy is considered only when a focal soft-tissue indication around the coccyx is identified and its alternatives and invasive risks have been discussed.

What does the evidence actually support?

A 2021 review recommends history and examination that distinguish trauma, morphology, dynamic instability and rare infection or tumour, with imaging when indicated. Dynamic imaging can identify hypermobility or dislocation in some patients, but measurement is not yet standardized.[1] A 2013 systematic review found only seven conservative-treatment studies with moderate to very-low-quality evidence and could not identify a single most effective intervention.[2] A 2020 laser-acupuncture paper reports one patient with refractory coccydynia after traumatic fracture.[3] Bodyall therefore does not repeat one intervention by default; pain location, sitting function and lumbar-pelvic movement are used to separate the roles of indicated conservative care.

Signs that need medical assessment first

Severe pain, deformity or difficulty walking after a major fall; fever, redness, drainage or a palpable mass; unexplained weight loss; and persistent night pain need investigation first. New bladder or bowel dysfunction, saddle numbness or progressive leg weakness requires immediate emergency assessment for possible cauda equina syndrome. Severe postpartum pain or associated rectal or pelvic symptoms should not delay the relevant specialist review.

Preparing for your first visit

Note a fall, childbirth, long drive or other trigger, the exact pain location, pain-free sitting time, change with rising or bowel movements, and any leg numbness, fever or skin change. Bring prior X-ray, CT or MRI results and a medication list.

What to write when booking

For example: “I fell two months ago; the tip of my coccyx hurts after 20 minutes of sitting and more when I stand.” Include the trigger, sitting time, pain location and neurological or systemic symptoms when choosing a time through Bodyall Korean Medicine Clinic booking in Suwon.

Key points

  • Tailbone pain is not presumed to result from pelvic malalignment.
  • Coccygeal tenderness, trauma and sitting load are distinguished from lumbar, sacroiliac, hip, pelvic-floor and neurological problems.
  • Chuna, SART and acupotomy are scoped only to an identified region and purpose.
  • The same sitting, sit-to-stand task and tenderness are retested; red flags prompt testing and specialist review first.
