Medial knee pain · pes anserine-region differential

Inner knee pain on stairs: what does Bodyall distinguish first?

Who may reasonably consider a Suwon consultation?

This page is for someone whose familiar pain sits slightly below the medial knee joint line and is triggered by stairs, longer walks or pressure on that spot. Location alone does not confirm pes anserine bursitis or tendinopathy. Bodyall asks about training changes, injury, swelling and locking, then compares local tenderness, active and passive knee motion, valgus stress, hip and ankle movement, gait and the painful stair or sit-to-stand task.

Pain location and the provoking task start the assessment; an image cannot establish the diagnosis or treatment choice.

1. How is pes anserine-region pain separated from other knee problems?

Clues worth assessing

  • Focal tenderness below the medial joint line
  • Familiar pain with stairs, running, longer walks or deep flexion
  • A recent increase in training, hills or repeated squatting

Other causes to check

  • Joint-line tenderness or locking: possible meniscal problem
  • Instability after trauma: possible medial collateral or other injury
  • Diffuse stiffness or swelling: osteoarthritis or an intra-articular cause
  • Tingling extending from the back: possible neural pain

A review notes that clinical features can be equivocal when distinguishing tendon and bursal pathology, so a single tender point is not treated as a final diagnosis.[2]

When testing or urgent assessment comes first

Seek appropriate urgent assessment for inability to bear weight or deformity after major trauma, a hot red swollen knee with fever, or sudden calf swelling with chest pain or breathlessness.[4]

3. Each treatment has a different job

Load and exercise guidance

Temporarily adjust sharp increases in stairs, running or squatting and progress thigh and hip movement or strength tasks. Recent review literature keeps conservative care first-line.[3]

Acupuncture or pharmacopuncture

May be considered for local symptom management after tenderness and function are assessed. The page does not promise tendon regeneration or prevention of recurrence.

Myofascial-release Chuna

Used when adductor, hamstring or other soft-tissue tension and movement restriction contribute to the painful task, with the same task retested.

Corrective Chuna/SART

Not automatic for medial knee pain. It is considered only when examination finds a relevant, modifiable hip, pelvic, ankle or weight-bearing pattern. It does not mean permanent pelvic restoration or guaranteed knee-pressure reduction.

Acupotomy

Considered only for selected persistent focal restriction after alternatives and need are discussed. Bleeding, bruising, infection and possible injury to nerves, vessels or tendon mean it is not routine care for every case.

4. What the evidence does—and does not—show

A 2007 case-control study compared 22 patients with 38 controls and found an association with valgus knee, but its confidence intervals were wide and it did not test pelvic torsion or SART.[1] A 2010 review described diagnostic ambiguity and major gaps in etiology and pathophysiology.[2] A 2025 review of knee bursae kept conservative management first-line while noting heterogeneous protocols and the need for better trials.[3]

These sources support careful localization, differential assessment and staged conservative care. They do not prove Bodyall-specific outcomes or superiority over another clinic.

Common patient questions

Does pain below the inner knee definitely mean pes anserine bursitis?

No. Location and stair pain are clues, but meniscal, medial collateral ligament, osteoarthritis and neural causes also need assessment. Bodyall compares history, knee and hip movement, gait and local findings.

Is SART required for pes anserine-region pain?

No. Load adjustment, local care and myofascial-release Chuna may come first when the problem is primarily local. Corrective Chuna/SART is considered only when a relevant and modifiable hip, pelvic, ankle or weight-bearing pattern is found.

How is the treatment response checked?

The same stair, walking or sit-to-stand task and the original tender point are checked again after treatment. If the response is absent or the symptom pattern changes, the working explanation and plan are reassessed.
