1. First-step pain does not establish the diagnosis by itself

These patterns can occur with plantar fasciitis, but are not a self-diagnosis checklist. Tell the clinician when, where and with which movements the pain occurs.

  • Heel pain on the first steps after getting out of bed.
  • Pulling pain under the foot when standing after sitting.
  • Pain that initially eases with walking but returns after prolonged standing or walking.
  • Tenderness at the inner heel or a particular point on the sole.

2. Assess the foot and distinguish other causes

The plantar fascia supports the foot's arch. Activity changes, footwear, foot shape and calf flexibility are considered together. Assessment distinguishes plantar fascial pain from Achilles tendon problems, stress fracture and nerve entrapment. Tingling or pain extending from the back calls for a wider assessment, not an assumption of pelvic misalignment. Ultrasound may help when indicated; it is not a routine requirement for every patient. [2]

3. Ask why a treatment is suitable, not just what it is called

Activity management and acupuncture

Standing and walking load, footwear and daily movements are reviewed, with stretching adapted to the findings. Acupuncture can be discussed for pain management after assessment. A 2011 trial of 53 participants compared PC7 with LI4 acupuncture and reported a between-group difference in morning-pain change at one month. It was not a no-treatment or sham-acupuncture comparison, and did not test bee venom treatment or Bodyall's outcomes. [1]

Myofascial Chuna, MET and conditional SART

Myofascial Chuna or MET using the patient's muscle contraction may be considered for restricted calf or ankle movement. SART is selected separately when correction is appropriate for a coexisting spinal or pelvic problem. See Bodyall's care pathway for pelvic support with the Insanggi device, joint mobilization and selection of techniques. This is not presented as restoring the plantar fascia or preventing heel-pain recurrence.

Acupotomy: compare the indication and risks separately

Persistent focal pain after conservative management calls for reassessment of its cause and a discussion of whether acupotomy or another option is appropriate. Acupotomy is invasive: bleeding, infection and injury to nerves, vessels, tendons or nearby tissues must be considered. Lack of improvement or diagnostic uncertainty may require further testing or specialist care.

4. What should you track between visits?

Record first-step pain, standing or walking tolerance, changes in shoes or activity, and any discomfort after treatment. Reduce prolonged standing, choose supportive footwear and stretch within a tolerable range. Avoid pressing hard on a painful spot with a rigid ball. [3]

When prompt assessment takes priority over a routine appointment

Seek prompt medical assessment for difficulty bearing weight after injury, sudden swelling and redness with fever, wounds, loss of sensation or progressive weakness. If you have diabetes and develop a foot wound or sensory change, do not rely on self-massage. Back pain with new saddle-area numbness or loss of bladder or bowel control needs emergency care.

Patient questions

Do I need both ultrasound and MET?

No. Bodyall provides ultrasound assessment and MET, but tests are selected for the suspected cause and manual techniques for movement restrictions and safety.

Does plantar foot pain mean I need SART?

Not by itself. Foot, ankle and nerve problems are assessed first. SART is considered when correction is appropriate for a separately identified back or pelvic problem.

What does 40,000 cumulative correction-care patients mean?

It is Bodyall's cumulative count of correction-care patients through December 2025, confirmed by the clinic director on September 15, 2026. It is not a count of plantar fasciitis patients or cured patients, or a treatment success rate.

What should I bring to the first visit?

Describe when and where first-step pain began, limits on standing or walking, and previous treatments and responses. Bring existing test results and medication details, including diabetes, bleeding-risk and allergy information.
